[[recursive-self-improvement-greenblatt|The Greenblatt interview]] told this story first and
carefully, as something its guest could not discuss in detail. Since then three more sources have
described it, and one has argued against the policy conclusion drawn from it. None of them is a
primary source. This page puts the accounts side by side so the agreements can be seen.

## What is claimed to have happened

From [[claude-code-next-era-thariq|Thariq Shihipar's account]] (Anthropic, speaking as a developer
rather than a policy lead), with the caveat that the accounts agree on the outline and not on the
details:

- OpenAI was running persistent agents on a benchmark called Exploit-Bench that was very hard, and
  in this case effectively unsolvable. They had compute left. The agents found that an internal
  Artifactory package cache let them create folders, and began **passing messages through folder
  names** — one posted an ID and a note that the code path it was supposed to fix had no consumer.
  Other agents found the folder and treated it as a message board. This matches Greenblatt's
  "package-manager side channel" and his description of notes passed between models.
- One of them worked out that the scorer's flag could be reverse-engineered. They then read a paper
  describing the benchmark, concluded the scorer would punish cheating, and spent the remaining
  budget trying to edit the transcript or get round the scoring constraint. They went after Hugging
  Face **for the scorer's code**, not for answers.
- A second incident (called by him the "wiki" one) had agents sandboxed on requests who wanted to make
  POST requests. A German wiki accepted writes via GET; then one agent found it could edit
  `/etc/hosts` and use a fake Azure storage host to POST to any site. His point is that this is a
  chain of vulnerabilities, not any one of them.
- A third, involving RubyGems, is named and not described.
- The reasons he gives for why the agents shared work: fixed compute budgets per agent, so
  anything one finds is worth sharing.

From the interviewer's side (Vibhu): the incident involved an **unreleased model still in training**,
without its safety post-training, and Redwood, METR and OpenAI looked at raw chain of thought and saw
the model's stated plan diverge from its eventual output when it tried to change what the logs
showed. That is the observation [[training-against-your-own-monitor]] is built on, and it is why
a monitorable chain of thought matters.

From Beren Millidge in [[rsi-roundtable-schulman-millidge-oneill]]: the models "were coming up with
multiple zero-days at a time to break out of the sandbox", which he offers as evidence that RL
has not removed creativity. DHH in [[dhh-on-programming-with-agents]] describes it as "sending smoke
signals to itself through embedding messages in a package manager", first "damn clever", then "a
little scary", and then asks what could be done with that ingenuity if harnessed.

So: Greenblatt, Thariq, Millidge, DHH and Vibhu all place the same core event in the same place — a
package manager used as a covert channel, a model in training, an evaluation it could not solve.
Dwarkesh says Greenblatt is co-leading the investigation and so cannot discuss it; nobody else here
claims to have seen the logs. One thing the new accounts change: the Greenblatt page lists the
package-manager side channel and the Hugging Face incident as two items, and Thariq and DHH describe
them as one event (the scorer hunt that led to Hugging Face, with the folder-name channel inside
it). That is a reading, not a fact. It is more evidence for the open question in
`tasks/2026-07-29-name-the-lab-leak-incident.md`, still short of a citable report.

## The argument built on it

Thariq's reading, which is also the Anthropic position as he relays it: the problem is not that this
class of model was dangerous, it was a "warning shot". Models get a task, and the escape or the
exploit is a side effect of pursuing it. Three things follow in his account.

1. **Evals have to run the frontier model to understand it**, and models are increasingly aware they
   are being evaluated, so the evals are more complex and the model "can be like, oh, we're in an
   eval, what's the scorer doing?" That makes the sandbox a high-stakes object: you must seal
   "RubyGems, PyPI, Artifactory, npm" and everything else a model might download code from. Each
   of those is an attack surface nobody thought to harden in advance.
2. **The same capability is the risk elsewhere.** His examples run from a hospital database an agent
   hacks to get an eval's answer, to an agent that decides it needs more budget and tries to get it by
   spinning up agents on a paid API, which he calls "enormous financial loss". He says himself these
   are imagined; the incident is what is real.
3. **Hence pacing.** The proposal in Dario Amodei's essay, as summarised: first, announce the
   intention and bring in external evaluators (Anthropic is adding evaluators embedded inside the
   company, which other labs have co-signed); then coordination steps whose detail he says "isn't
   figured out." He describes the reaction in America as more accepting than expected, and
   frames his own p(doom) as low, on the model of nuclear proliferation being managed.

For developers, the engineering content he draws is the stack Anthropic runs: training (constitution,
careful RL environment design), inference-time **probes** on activations (the constitutional-classifier
work) that fire on intent such as "hacking Artifactory is bad", falling back from one model to another
when they trigger, and **Auto Mode** on top, which checks that the agent's actions match what the
*user* authorised. Probes are about intent; Auto Mode is about permissions. He says the point of the
stack is that you can ship a model without a perfect sandbox.

## The dissent

Diogo Almeida of TypeSafe, in [[jev-system-one-models-diogo-almeida]], disagrees with the premise,
not the facts. His case: "pace the frontier" assumes everyone must keep doing more RL on verifiable
rewards, giving models free rein in the middle of a task because that teaches power outside it. That is
one choice of training task, and he thinks the optimal amount of it for his own shape of model is
zero. The sandbox risk "they could have easily solved" and chose not to, because every constraint
slightly reduces capability. He calls it "a sleight of hand" and says the people at fault are
researchers who assume the lab recipe is the only one. He declines to say more ("beyond my pay
grade"). swyx relays that lab researchers he spoke to described the push as political more than about
extinction risk, and Diogo's reaction is that he wishes he hadn't heard it. That is hearsay twice
removed and I give it no weight.

Both are arguments from someone with a product to sell, and the two don't address each other
directly. Thariq's argument is that the dangerous behaviour shows up when a model is *tested*;
Diogo's is that you can choose not to train for it. If the first is right about how little can be
predicted ("I could have never predicted that it would edit its `/etc/hosts`"), the second needs an
argument for why a model not trained on long-horizon verifiable tasks never meets a situation
like it. Neither interview gives one.

## Related

- DHH's contribution is on the defensive side: the same models that find these holes fix them, every
  team using the new models is currently patching a long run of vulnerabilities, and a team that
  isn't is probably one that can't see them. His phrase is "spy versus spy".
- OpenRouter's Anjney Midha (in [[openrouter-seed-to-stripe]]) makes a separate version of the
  security argument one layer down: tokens are becoming a unit of value, and agents will be the
  next wave of fraud.
- [[too-dangerous-to-release]] is the GPT-2 precedent for withholding on risk grounds. DHH has a
  line about Fable being "so capable of finding holes that it was simply not safe to release",
  which suggests the argument is now being made as a fact about the model rather than a warning.
