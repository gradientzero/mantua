Source: [Dwarkesh Podcast, "AI researchers debate how close we are to recursive self-improvement"](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/john-beren-charlie),
2026-09-11, captured 2026-10-05 and archived under
`sources/2026-10/dwarkesh-rsi-roundtable-schulman-millidge-oneill/`. The three guests are John
Schulman (chief scientist at Thinking Machines, formerly OpenAI), Beren Millidge (CTO of Zyphra)
and Charlie O'Neill (head of model training at Baseten). Dwarkesh picked them because they work
at open-ish labs and can say things on the record. The capture is the page's own transcript.

This is the second source here on [[recursive-self-improvement-greenblatt|whether the loop closes
on itself]], and the first where the speakers are the people who would build the loop. It reads as
a long answer to one question: what would have to be true for 2036 to look normal?

## The part that is nearly mechanical

O'Neill's distinction does most of the work. When the objective is already written down — lower
pre-training loss, a higher score on a fixed environment — thinking harder buys a lot. His
examples are errors that a patient reader could have caught in the original scaling-law papers:
Kaplan's fit ignored learning-rate annealing and the embedding parameters, and muP and
width-dependent learning rates were findable by reasoning. He would "imagine a 10× speed-up if our
thing is just 'maximise the objective we're currently on'." Schulman expects a future where AI
spends compute on analysis and theory comparable to what it spends on experiments, and says "we're
nowhere near the ceiling of how well you can do research."

That is the verifiable-research half of Greenblatt's case, said by people with a closer view of it.
Nobody here disputes it.

## The part nobody has shown

Choosing the objective is where they hold back. Millidge: "How well can AIs generalise to learning
their own objectives?" A self-propelling loop has to propose an objective, optimise it, propose the
next, and not go off the rails for a long time. Schulman's guess for the last human job is the same
point from the other side: defining what we want, which in a post-training team means deciding how
the model should behave in each area. O'Neill adds that research which shifts a paradigm is the
open-ended kind, where "the AIs are definitely not able to specify that objective either."

Schulman also notes that a lot of real research is not hill-climbing. It is an intuition, a task
designed to show signs of life for it, and then successively more realistic versions. The inner
loop tests the intuition; it does not hand it over. That is a harder thing to put in a container
than [[containerising-ai-research|the three scales of environment]] in the Greenblatt material,
and the roundtable does not pretend otherwise.

## The Moore's-law worry

O'Neill's version of the case against: the current recipe (transformer, RL, more environments) may
have an optimum not far above it, and every apparent straight line in this field has been held
straight by discrete inventions. Pre-training scaling hit diminishing returns, RL took over, and
that bought a fresh curve. If the next step needs another discontinuity of that size, an LLM
trained on RL environments, even ones aimed at self-improvement, may not find it. Dwarkesh's reply
is the chess-Elo one: capability rises linearly in Elo and the practical consequence is a cliff when
the machine crosses the human range. Millidge agrees it would be strange for things to asymptote
just short of that, and says regulation is the likelier reason for an unremarkable 2036 than
anything technical.

## Which tasks close and which don't

The most reusable idea in the episode is O'Neill's split between **cumulative** and
**non-stationary** tasks. Self-improvement is cumulative: once attention, mixture-of-experts or
GRPO is discovered it goes into the training stack and stays. His example is a recent frontier
training run that "didn't have to go back and discover attention" and could call the existing
`pre-training.sh`. A legal associate is the opposite, because the people, relationships and local
habits it has to hold keep changing. Dwarkesh: "It's so unfortunate that RSI happened to be easier
than being a paralegal." If labs believe that, effort drifts toward the cumulative tasks first.

That fits what [[jaggedness-and-what-rl-optimises]] says from Karpathy's side. The shape of the
improvement follows what can be scored and what can be accumulated.

## How the first automated researchers get trained

Schulman's guess: some mix of human feedback to absorb researchers' taste and many practice
environments of multi-step research projects, patched each iteration where researchers notice the
model is weak. O'Neill describes the lab practice as working at the leading edge of the lineage:
take the bugs found in the training stack over the last few months and turn them into
environments. He notes this is why some of them feel the curve is asymptotic — "you're always just
trying to get the last three months of progress." Millidge's answer is that environments can
exceed humans where trajectories cannot: you can build one nobody can solve. Rolling the lineage
back to before GRPO and letting the model rediscover things is possible in principle and too
expensive in practice.

## Continual learning, and why it isn't one switch

This is the section that bears most on [[learning-from-deployment]] and on
[[model-speciation-and-touching-the-weights]].

- Millidge: deployment data already feeds the next generation, through mid-training or filtering.
  Labs say they don't train on users' data; he is sure the Chinese labs do, and calls that "basically
  what distillation is" (see [[distillation-and-who-keeps-up]]).
- Schulman: whether a deployed model learns across all its customers is largely an incentive
  question. Companies will not want the provider to learn from their traffic and erode their
  advantage.
- O'Neill: so the economics push toward modules that get swapped in — a LoRA, or a "cartridge", a
  KV cache trained to be very compressed — rather than updates to one shared model. Millidge's
  answer is that the provider can still take the traces and fold them into the next pre-train, so
  the move is staged: specialise, collect traces, consolidate, release, with the cadence
  shortening from every three months towards every week.
- Examples of the small, human-in-the-loop version already running: Cursor's Composer, trained with
  online REINFORCE on real use and redeployed about every five hours if it improved on their own
  benchmark; Harvey doing the same for legal agents. O'Neill's difficulty with this is variance
  (one user, one rollout, no group), and Schulman's is the reward itself: "did they accept the
  edit" is a superficial signal and gets hacked.
- Why it breaks at the micro level: SFT on successful traces, even on-policy distillation, shows
  catastrophic forgetting once you do hundreds of small updates. RL is gentler because it changes
  little, which also limits how much you can put in with it, and it is better at capabilities than
  at specific facts. Millidge: it is easy to distil into a *different* base model, and continually
  mid-training the same base asymptotes. So "keep training the same model forever" has no good
  method yet, and "retrain from scratch on everything" is the fallback and is expensive.

The practical reading for anything built here: the near-term route to a model that knows my
context is the context window plus a small attached module, not the weights. That is the position
the speciation note already attributes to Karpathy, and here three people at three labs give the
mechanism.

## The data ladder

On how far data gets you, O'Neill's picture is a ladder of environments in which each rung is
exponentially harder to build. Two data points on its steepness:

- Schulman cites someone who fine-tuned the "Talkie" model, pre-trained only on text up to 1930,
  on modern coding-agent data, and it beat Claude 3 Opus on SWE-bench. Once there is an example of
  expert behaviour, it is cheap to copy into a weak model.
- O'Neill's counter: a paper that trained a model up to fifth-grade maths and then tried RL on
  college maths, and could not get it to climb at all. Climbing in year-by-year steps worked. Millidge
  puts the reason as the exploration limit: if the model cannot get an answer in 128 rollouts it
  gets no signal, which is why RL needs curricula and pre-training does not.

The source of new bits is the problem. In pre-training the signal is already in Common Crawl and
the work is filtering. Later, "there's no hidden proof of a Millennium Prize problem sitting in
Common Crawl". The bits have to come from humans writing out reasoning, from humans designing
environments, or from deployment.

Dwarkesh's own experiment (with Jerry Han, at small scale) crossed every training recipe from 2019
on with every dataset from 2019 on. Data accounted for about a 12× compute-efficiency gain and
architecture about 3.7×. The combined ~33× is well short of the ~2,000× implied by the 3×-a-year
trend (3 to the seventh power) that Epoch estimated, so either the rest is post-training or the
gains are scale-dependent and the experiment was too small to see them. Millidge pushes back on
the framing: an architecture change such as grouped-query attention lets you reach a regime (long
context) where the data in question is usable, so measuring it at 2K context makes data look more
important than it is. This is the [[data-versus-architecture]] question again, with a number and a
caveat.

## Why RL works at all

Dwarkesh restates the pessimistic case (about one bit per episode, almost none when pass rates are
low). Millidge's answer has three parts. Mid-training on synthetic reasoning data does a large
share of the work — he says roughly 80% of the way to the final RL checkpoint — so RL only has to
adjust a policy. The bit it does get is high signal because the objective ignores the rest of the
trace, unlike SFT, which has to match every token. And one bit can still change function space a
lot. O'Neill adds that what generalised was not reasoning across domains (maths does not make you a
coder) but *horizon*: models learned to keep going on a task, and a paper he cites (EdgeBench)
measured that rate doubling every three months. A third explanation is quanta: each task is a phase
transition on its own, and averaged across enough tasks it looks smooth.

On creativity, Schulman's point is that RL has cut the diversity of outputs. Models reuse themes
and character names, and because many labs distil from Claude, open-weight models now write with the
same tics, which he finds worrying. Millidge puts the blame on the judge being exploited, not on RL.

## Timelines

Rapid fire, with each person's answer:

| | Schulman | Millidge | O'Neill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drop-in remote worker, a month of work, general white-collar | about a year, in some form | about 3 years for full generality; 80–90% sooner | about a year if not browser-bound, a couple if it is |
| 10× uplift to AI researchers | 2 years | roughly agrees with 2 | 5–10 years |
| Beats top humans on all computer work (ASI) | 3–4 years | about 5 | 5–10 |

O'Neill's 5–10 years for researchers is longer than his answer for the remote worker, and Dwarkesh
realises they are defining the remote worker differently (ordinary white-collar work over about a
month, not a researcher). For O'Neill the crux on research is his own capacity to absorb information and choose the next experiment. Millidge's retort is that
the model can run two or three experiments in a row without crashing, which would already be a
large uplift.

## Against what is here

**It runs somewhat later than Greenblatt.** His medians are full automation of AI R&D in 2030–31
and "beats all humans" around 2033. Schulman's 3–4 years for the last is earlier than that, Millidge's
five about the same, and O'Neill's 5–10 later. The structural difference is not the dates. Greenblatt
puts the weight on research being verifiable enough to automate; these three agree on that and put
the open question on objective choice, continual learning and non-stationary work.

**Their uncertainty is about the same thing as the Karpathy material.** Both are about whether
anything outside a scorable domain moves. The roundtable adds a mechanism for why it might not, in the
exploration limit and the cost of building rungs.

**The side-channel incident turns up again.** Millidge uses the OpenAI/Hugging Face incident as an
example of "move 37" creativity, since the models chained several zero-days to leave a sandbox. See
[[pacing-the-frontier-agents-and-sandboxes]] for the three accounts of it now in the notebook.
