## The multi-model bet

The objection they met in 2023 was "big model wins": scaling laws and network effects would give one
company a Google-style monopoly. The investor's answer was that scaling laws were a feature, since they
meant many labs with enough compute would each get somewhere, and that he had already backed Mistral,
Black Forest Labs and Luma. Atallah's origin story is practical: he saw the Alpaca result, a Llama
fine-tuned for about $600 that was hard to tell from ChatGPT in use, and concluded that models were
cheap to make and there was nowhere to *see* them used. Hugging Face had no closed models and no usage
data.

The investor's own route to the idea was Discord, where he ran the platform: a moderation bot built on
an early OpenAI model refused to moderate Harry Potter fan servers, because the prompt tripped trademark
guardrails. Detective-story authors got refusals on a violent chapter. Discord asked for the weights to
get reliability and was told no. That is the same argument as the refusals material in
[[jev-system-one-models-diogo-almeida]], arrived at from an enterprise deployment.

## Why labs lose their distribution

The investor's claim, from seeing several labs launch: researchers think in capabilities, finish a
checkpoint after spending a great deal, release an API, and then "there'd be crickets". The first
Claude release had three developer examples. Mistral's first checkpoint was a torrent link. Googlers
can push a new checkpoint to billions of devices overnight, which is the advantage a router replaces
for everybody else: key management, endpoint versioning, billing, discovery. A neutral third party can
also describe a model honestly, since models are black boxes, including open-weight ones.

## The pattern in the leaderboard

Atallah's description of what the rankings showed is a cycle. A lab ships a frontier jump, usage
surges, users look at the invoice thirty days later, and an open-weight model arrives two or three
months later as an alternative. He saw it several times. Milestones he gives: coding was not usable
before Claude 3.5 Sonnet in mid-2024; the Mistral 8×7B launch in late 2023 was the first clear case of
a provider marketplace adding value, with hosts competing on price and, in swyx's words, the price falling about 80%;
at the end of 2025 the OpenClaw agent brought in non-developers, and its heartbeat design (calling a
chosen model every thirty minutes just to check it is alive) made the auto router useful; Hermes then
leaned into skills and memory. A side remark in the Mistral story: the model felt smarter than
it measured partly because it was fast, "so humans are the router" until the router existed.

Volume is now about ten trillion tokens a day, growing about
9% a week by token volume (swyx's figure, which Atallah says sounds right), with over 10 million developers by an uncleaned count.

## Roads not taken

They prototyped and dropped a tuning service (give it YouTube videos and it tunes a model to talk like
the person), and did not build tuning, memory, skills or sandboxes, because developers have varied
preferences for those and a neutral marketplace can partner with the providers instead. Atallah's
position on focus is that the world has to be able to say what you are known for. The
investor's example: Anthropic's founding memo was about an AI pair programmer, which is why they
declined image and video models, and the main evals were coding from day one.

**Fusion.** In early 2024 they built "MOM", a mixture of models that fused three models' answers with
a fourth, and deleted it: the best model was so far ahead that the fused output matched it. Years
later they rebuilt it because the top three or four models are now close and complementary. His own
test: ask all models for an architecture plan, fuse the plans, and ask each whether the fused one beats
its own; they all said yes, repeated a few times. That is a self-judged result and the sort the
[[generator-evaluator-loops]] material says to distrust, so the benchmark they went on to build matters.

## Why Stripe

The honest answer is fraud. Atallah says the dollar volume of fraud they blocked in the last month was
ten times that of the month before, across stolen cards, resold inference against the terms of
service, hacked or compromised accounts, and runaway agents. If you run a gateway selling
general inference you are a target; if you sell a narrow task you are much less one. He expects
companies to move from reselling inference with a markup to charging for discrete tasks and letting
customers bring their own inference.

The investor's version is longer. When he ran Discord's platform, Midjourney's free trial was resold
from China overnight and the trial was shut; he concluded that tokens were a new unit of value on the
internet and that fraud would follow value. His numbers are projections, not data: a token economy of
about $5 trillion within five years and $10 trillion in ten, fraud scaling with it, and the next wave
coming "not just from humans but from AI agents". Stripe's fit, he argues, is Radar: it ate fraud up
front as acquisition cost, then built the data to detect it.

## What it adds here

- [[kv-cache-economics-of-agents]] describes the bill the router sees. A gateway is where per-step
  routing happens, and the per-task versus per-step distinction in that note applies directly to the
  auto router: a router that picks a model per request loses cache on every switch.
- It is a data point for what [[inference-engineering-baseten]] calls the customer side of the
  counter: the neutral layer takes a cut of every token.
- OpenRouter's stated data policy is that it can't see prompts or completions unless an organisation
  opts in. That is relevant to [[distillation-and-who-keeps-up]], where a different class of proxy
  service is described as selling traffic. Nothing links the two, and I don't claim they are the same.
- Agents as consumers: the investor's "pub-sub" framing (humans consume ad hoc, agents continuously
  and from changing SKUs) is [[the-customer-is-not-the-human]] stated as a marketplace design principle.

## Caveats

It is a friendly conversation between a founder and an investor about a company that has just been
sold, so the origin story is polished. The fraud figures are a month-over-month ratio with no base.
The $7B price and the 10-trillion-token figure come from the page's standfirst and from the guests,
and I haven't checked either.
