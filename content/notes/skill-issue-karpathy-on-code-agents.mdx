## The through-line

Karpathy says that in December something flipped for him — from writing roughly 80% of his
code by hand to delegating roughly 80% of it, and by March to not having typed a line of
code since. What interests him is not the capability jump itself but the position it leaves
the operator in: when a task fails, his default reading is that he did not string the
available pieces together well enough. Bad instructions in the agents file, no decent
memory tool, not enough sessions in parallel. Hence the phrase the episode is named for.

The same framing shows up as a resource anxiety worth recording because it is unusual and
specific: he describes feeling nervous when he has subscription quota left over at the end
of a period, because unused quota means he did not find enough work to delegate. He draws
the parallel to being a PhD student with idle GPUs. The metric he is optimising is his own
token throughput.

## The hedge that matters

Late in the interview, having laid out the progression he expects, he stops and says that
the whole thing is still bursting at the seams, that going too far ahead makes it net not
useful, and then — about whether that is a limitation of the models or of the operator —
that it is hard to tell.

That hedge deserves to be written down next to the slogan, because "everything is a skill
issue" is otherwise unfalsifiable. If every failure is provisionally the operator's fault,
no amount of failure counts as evidence about the tools. Karpathy is more careful than the
slogan is: he reports getting frustrated with agents that do nonsensical things, and he
does not claim to know which side the fault sits on. The useful version of the phrase is
narrower — *assume it is your harness first, because that is the part you can change* — and
that is how [[the-harness-is-a-skill-issue|the owner uses it]] too.

## Three side arguments worth keeping

**Software demand goes up, not down.** Asked why engineering demand is still rising, his
answer is Jevons: software was scarce because it was expensive, the price is falling, so
the latent demand shows up. He is cautiously optimistic on this and says explicitly that
forecasting it is an economist's job, not his. The canonical example he reaches for is ATMs
and bank tellers — cheaper branches, so more branches, so more tellers.

**Digital first, atoms later, and the interesting part is the seam.** He expects a large
amount of reorganisation in purely digital work, because there is an overhang of digital
information nobody had the thinking cycles to process, and much slower movement in
robotics, on the grounds that his self-driving experience says physical autonomy takes far
more capital and far more time than anyone budgets. The part he finds most interesting is
neither end but the interface: sensors that feed the system and actuators that let it act.
His examples of "sensors" are deliberately broad — lab equipment for materials research,
paid human data collection, and the observation that there is no market where you can pay
ten dollars for a photograph from a specific place. He gestures at Daniel Suarez's *Daemon*
for the version of that where people are the actuators.

**Being outside a frontier lab costs you calibration.** Asked why he is not doing this at a
lab with a cluster, he gives both sides. Against: financial alignment with the thing you
are supposed to be assessing, things you cannot say, and no real control over the
organisation's decisions when the stakes rise. For: the labs are opaque, they are the ones
who know what is coming, and he expects his own judgment to drift outside. He is explicit
that this is a reason to go back for a while.

That last point is the load-bearing premise of the owner's note — we are reading the labs'
work two to four months late, and the gap is the problem. Karpathy, who used to work at
one, says the same thing about himself.
