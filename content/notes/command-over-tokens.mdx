It is easy to say: if only I had limitless token expenditure, like inside one of the frontier
labs, I would be able to do so much more.

In practice I rarely see anyone exhaust their weekly limit on a pro subscription while
coding — let alone on knowledge work. It does happen at the session level, especially with
high reasoning effort and several sessions at once, but not across a whole week.

And having a high token throughput that is actually *meaningful* is a skill in itself.
[[skill-issue-karpathy-on-code-agents|Karpathy talks about exactly this]] as a skill issue.

So you read the examples from
[[harness-design-for-long-running-coding-agents|Anthropic]] and
[[harness-engineering-agent-first-repositories|OpenAI]] about long-running agents, and you
wonder whether something like [[auto-research|Auto Research]] already exists — whether you can
formulate a wish, a specification, and have it run for hours or days until the task is done.
Technically that is possible, but not in the way you think. It is a skill issue.

And nobody outside the labs knows what they really run. What is published is what they chose to
publish; the experiments themselves are months old by the time we read them, and now and then
something leaks out that suggests there is more.

So we are left to our own devices, which is also very empowering. Nobody knows exactly how to
use these tools yet. It is always a play between model capability and the harness. And with
good skills and good harness design you can let an agent do some magical things for you.

## Why the allowance is not the constraint

Two things sit behind the observation above, and they pull in the same direction.

The first is that an idle subscription is a diagnosis, not a saving. Karpathy's version is
that unspent quota means he failed to maximise throughput, the way idle GPUs used to mean a
PhD student was wasting flops. The binding constraint moved from the compute to the operator.

The second is that spend on its own says nothing about return. DoorDash burned about 20× more
in June than in January and then had to
[[benchmarking-your-own-agent-spend|build a benchmark over their own tasks]] to find out what
it bought. Volume was never the achievement. What you want is not more tokens spent but more
tokens spent on work you can verify — which is exactly what a harness is for.

So the practical question is not how to get a larger allowance. It is: what would I have to
build so that a week's worth of tokens could actually be consumed on something I trust?

## Four decisions that make a harness

Everything below is a decision, not a component. A harness is the set of things you decided
the model cannot do alone yet — and Anthropic's own framing is the test to keep:

> every component in a harness encodes an assumption about what the model can't do on its own,
> and those assumptions are worth stress testing

They are worth stress testing because they may be wrong, and because they go stale on the next
model release. When you take one out, take out one at a time — their radical one-shot
simplification failed and left them unable to tell which pieces had been load-bearing.

### 1. Decide what the agent can see

An agent's world is what it can reach in-context while running. OpenAI's line for it is that
anything it cannot access effectively does not exist: Slack threads and shared docs are
invisible, repository-local versioned artifacts are the whole world. That turns "write it
down" from hygiene into a capability decision.

Concretely, and cheapest first: a short entry-point document that is a table of contents
rather than a manual, the docs it points at kept current by a recurring gardening pass, and
the app or the build made runnable by the agent itself so it can check its own change. A
hundred lines that route attention beat a thousand that crowd the context out.

### 2. Decide what "done" means before anything runs

This is where throughput is actually won or lost, because an unverifiable run has to be
reviewed by hand, and hand review is the thing that does not scale with tokens.

Make the criteria measurable. "Does it look visually pleasing" is not concrete and models
struggle with it. "Is the colour scheme one of these", "is the spacing appropriate — nothing
too narrow or too wide", "does it scale across window sizes", "are all the buttons clickable"
can be verified. The Anthropic harness goes one step further and has the generator and the
evaluator negotiate a contract for the chunk of work — what will be built, and how success
gets checked — before any code is written. One of their sprint contracts had 27 test criteria.

Then decide who fetches the evidence. A grader that reads the transcript is marking the
generator's homework: the verdict is independent, the evidence is not. A grader that drives
the running app itself, or reads the logs and metrics, is the version that catches
display-only features. [[keeping-an-agent-running|`/goal`]] is the free, weaker form of this,
and useful precisely where the end state is mechanical — a clean build, no new warnings.

### 3. Decide what is enforced mechanically

The separate evaluator exists because agents are generous about their own work. But a rubric
is not the only place to put a standard, and it is the most expensive one — it costs a model
call and a round trip.

Anything that can be a lint should be a lint. OpenAI enforces layering and dependency
direction, structured logging, naming, file-size limits with agent-written linters, and the
detail worth copying is that the *error messages carry the remediation instructions* — the
guardrail teaches the agent in the moment it trips.
[[linting-as-agent-guardrail|Linting as agent guardrail]] has the mechanics. Here, `npm run
build` is the test and `/lint` is the health check, which is a small version of the same idea.

Their other observation is the one to plan for: agents replicate whatever patterns exist in
the repo, including the bad ones, so drift is structural rather than occasional. Friday
cleanup did not scale; a scheduled pass that opens small refactor PRs did.

### 4. Decide what starts the next turn, and what stops the sequence

The smallest part of a harness, the one that ships with the tool, and the cheapest place to
start: `/goal` restarts on a finished turn and stops on a model's verdict, `/loop` on an
interval, a Stop hook on your own script, a scheduled run on a clock with no session open in
between. [[keeping-an-agent-running]] is the table.

This is also where the parallelism decision lives. Karpathy's own practice is not one long
unattended run but many short ones — tasks sized to roughly twenty minutes at high effort,
several in flight, review proportional to how much you care about that particular code. Both
regimes are real and they are not the same workflow: one asks for good task decomposition, the
other for a grader you trust.

## How to get there without pretending

Two habits, and they are the same two I would give anyone starting.

**Run the loops by hand first.** Be the evaluator yourself for a few rounds before you write
one. You cannot specify a rubric for a judgement you have not made.

**Then read what the evaluator produces.** Out of the box the model is a poor QA agent — it
finds real issues and rationalises approving them, and it tests shallowly. If it is not
finding anything you would not have found easily yourself, it is not worth running yet, and
the fix is to read its logs, find where its judgement diverged from yours, and patch the
prompt. Several rounds of that.

And one thing not to do: **don't watch the agent work.** Let it run and come back on your own
terms. Watching breaks the deep work cycle and turns the session into a dopamine trigger. That
is not just hygiene — sitting in front of one run is the single most reliable way to keep your
throughput at one run.

## What this is really about

The wish is for a bigger allowance. The constraint is a harness good enough that the tokens
could be spent on work you would trust unwatched. Those are not the same problem, and only one
of them is mine to solve.

Which is the encouraging part. [[the-harness-is-a-skill-issue|It is a skill issue]] — and a
skill issue is a thing you can practise.
