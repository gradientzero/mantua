Source: [Latent Space, "Jev: System One models for Prod, not God — with Diogo Almeida, CEO, TypeSafe AI"](https://www.latent.space/p/jev),
2026-09-22, captured 2026-10-05 and archived under
`sources/2026-10/latent-space-jev-diogo-almeida/`. A two-and-a-half-hour interview by swyx, recorded
in launch week. The page's own intro is a long list of community links and use cases and is not
summarised here. Almeida co-authored InstructGPT and left OpenAI to start TypeSafe; Jev is the model
and the API.

**The author of the KV-cache post is the CEO of this company.** The "Diogo" of
[[kv-cache-economics-of-agents]] ("(KV) Cache Rules Everything Around Me", Substack *Complete Skeptic*)
is this person: the interview page links his X handle as @CompleteSkeptic and he says in it that he
wrote that article. The earlier note flagged that the post "ends by hinting at a new kind of
foundation model" and that he "has a stake". The stake is presumably Jev, though the interview does not
connect the two directly. What it does say is that he would like others to explore coding agents that
are "free from the tyranny of the KV cache", which he presents as a research agenda and not a Jev
feature.

## What Jev is

His one-line version: a new class of model "where the goal is for code to be the consumer." Pre-trained
models complete the internet, RLHF models answer a person, RLVR models optimise verifiable tasks. Jev's
output is meant to be parsed. TypeSafe's own name for the class is "System 1 models"; he is not
attached to it and says "decision model" is too narrow because the primitives will not all be
decisions.

It is optimised for **intelligence per dollar** (the name is Jevons). It deliberately has no reasoning
mode, and he says methods will follow the objective, so some lighter form of reasoning is "on the
cards". It has no vision in the current version, which Swyx raises as the first thing people want and
Diogo answers by joking, then says everything is in the cards.

Three API primitives, which he says map onto programming constructs:

| Primitive | Maps to | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| choice | switch on an enum | the closest thing to a function call |
| noulli (from Bernoulli) | `if` statement | a probability, not a boolean |
| score | sorting, thresholding | similar to LLM-as-judge |

Inputs are structured too: state, instructions and criteria are JSON objects the program slots in,
not text assembled into a system message. He calls system messages "disgusting global variables".

## The training claim: RLCD

TypeSafe's technique is "Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions", unpublished. His framing is
by north star, not by algorithm:

- RLHF's task is "please humans". It was never the PPO that mattered, he says, but the decision to
  optimise instruction-following. It brings mode collapse, hallucination, sycophancy and the style that
  wins on a chat arena.
- RLVR's task is "optimise benchmarks": anything programmatically verifiable is by definition a
  benchmark. It produces results on hard problems and a fragile, "fractal" jaggedness.
- RLCD's task is "make it reliable for programmatic use", with calibrated probabilities.

He is also explicit that TypeSafe is a data lab rather than a model lab ("model capabilities means
data"), that they avoid training on users' traffic because real traffic is a power law and would
overfit the model to the present (swyx says their data is all synthetic; Almeida says only that each
task has its own kind of data), and that he "wouldn't pre-train with $1 billion".
His mode-collapse argument for why strings are a bad decision medium: a calibrated model covering the
distribution is not punished for outliers, but a long string can only avoid errors by being very
conservative and over-confident. Compare [[jaggedness-and-what-rl-optimises]], where the same
boundary is drawn from the RL side.

## What he's strict about

**Reliability means robustness, not determinism.** Similar inputs should give similar outputs; they
test it by putting nonces (UUIDs) in prompts and wanting the same answer regardless. Determinism
could be offered but costs intelligence per dollar and GPU.

**No refusals.** The argument is that safety alignment belongs in a first-party product, and in an
API it is "a type error": a refusal in a dependency running in the background breaks someone else's
software, and the API provider should not know what the downstream task even is. "Intelligence will be
more like a database than a coworker." He also holds that every behavioural constraint "fractures" the
model's intelligence, and that a model should not be told it is Jev. He distinguishes capability
alignment (do what the user wants, welcome) from safety alignment (follow someone else's instructions).
A pushback from Swyx, that someone will use it in a war, gets the reply that he'll put his thumb on the
scale elsewhere but not at the technology layer. Set that against the layered stack Anthropic
describes in [[pacing-the-frontier-agents-and-sandboxes]].

**No public benchmarks.** He thinks they are gameable and the thing sold is a "je ne sais quoi" of
intelligence that has to be judged inside your own workflow. TypeSafe has internal evals and he calls
refusing to fool themselves a top-level rule. This hurt fundraising, he says. The honest limit is that,
without a benchmark, the "frontier of intelligence per dollar" is a claim customers must check.

**Versioning.** Models will not silently change after deployment. They will ship new versions fast
and may keep a long-term-support one for developers who depend on it, and he does not promise the
exact model forever. Swyx pushes him on this and on quantisation; the answer is a commitment not to
change deployed models, with LTS "research cooking."

## How to use it

The usable part for anyone, whether or not they use Jev, is in
[[decomposing-ai-work-into-small-decisions]]. In short: break work into the smallest semantic unit,
pass structure rather than prose, ask many cheap parallel questions instead of one large one, and put
the system's rules in code with thresholds and test cases.

His list of use families: *dark data* (data large companies hoard because running an LLM on it was too
expensive), real-time work where each 10 ms matters, *verify everything* (checking every LLM call, like
observability), and "smart software" (programming languages built on top of it). Computer use "came
from left field" and he is cautious about demos. He says Jev's niche is intelligence per dollar, not
per second, though speed is a real market.

Multi-hop reasoning is where swyx found it fell off ("single hop, state of the art; multi-hop starts
to fall down, monotonically as hops go up"). Diogo accepts that System 2 tasks should have low
confidence and heuristics may help.

## Coding agents beyond the KV cache

His closing task for others: coding agents that don't live inside a single model's cache.
The cache locks you to one model and rewards appending, so you can't practise normal state
management, can't hand an easier subtask to a cheaper model without passing all the state, and can't
let parallel agents read each other's state or coordinate with something smarter than locks. His list of
what that would unlock: sub-agents that return only what the parent needs, a searchable tree of
labelled subtasks, cheap lookup over history, and "Jev to solve locks". It is the constructive twin of
the argument in [[kv-cache-economics-of-agents]], and it is a research agenda rather than a result.

## Where it is and isn't corroborated

- OpenAI's response, an API built on its existing small model within a week, is in
  [[openai-devday-computer-use-and-decisions-api]]. It does not have the calibrated-confidence
  training; the interviewer there makes that point.
- The use-case claims (a trillion tokens a day, no marketing team, "more nines of uptime than Anthropic")
  are his, and a launch-week interview is the worst time to treat usage figures as settled.
- His account of OpenAI (ChatGPT as a copy of an internal Claude, being "disempowered" there, the
  November 2023 events) is a former employee's and largely unsupported here; I haven't used it.
- The statements about InstructGPT and the paper lineage are his recollection and the interviewer's;
  he hedges them himself.
