This is the first source in the notebook that isn't about agents, and it is worth reading on
its own terms before it is read as anything else. The one place it genuinely meets the rest
of the material is filed separately in [[separating-drafting-from-judging]].

Ferriss names four questions in the introduction, and they make a good enough spine:
how do you choose a project and stay with it, how do you begin when inspiration doesn't
show up, how do you build a practice that reliably produces pages, and how do you keep
going when you think the pages are terrible.

## Choosing, and staying

Gilbert's answer is the only one in the episode about *selection*, and it has two parts.

The first is a rule for the moment a better idea arrives mid-project — which she says
happens to her constantly, a quarter or a third of the way in. Her rule: you may abandon
the current project for the new one **only if you have a proven track record of finishing
things**. If you have thirty unfinished projects, the new idea is not a better idea, it is
the same event repeating. Her reasoning is that the new one will be stale in a few months
too, so the only thing the switch reliably produces is another unfinished project.

The second is a way of interrogating ideas before committing. She makes them pitch her —
"I'm the angel investor and these ideas are like, 'We want your time and money for this'" —
and reports that a lot of them evaporate under the question, because they turn out to be
"something about birds" rather than a project. The ones that survive get told to come back
when they are more formed.

Two things worth separating here. The pitch meeting is a real filter and it costs nothing to
run. The finishing rule is doing something different: it is a deliberate refusal to
re-evaluate on the merits, on the grounds that the in-flight judgement is known to be
corrupted. That is a strong move and she states the cost of it plainly — sometimes the new
idea really is better, and the rule tells you to ignore that.

## Beginning

Oates is the flattest on this and the most quotable. Ferriss reads her a line from a
*Paris Review* interview (The Art of Fiction No. 72) — "One must be pitiless about this
matter of 'mood.' In a sense, the writing will create the mood" — and asks her to expand. She declines to expand: "I definitely
feel that you create your mood by working." Don't wait to be inspired, don't overthink it,
go and work.

The part of her answer that is actually actionable is the second half, and it is about
protection rather than motivation. The problem is interruption; the people around you have
schedules and expectations, and "while you may love your family, they can be the ones who
drain you of energy." She is explicit that this falls hardest on women — the requests to read
manuscripts, sit on committees, work pro bono all arrive, and the instinct is to say yes. She
says yes to a great many of them, names Philip Roth and Flaubert as the counter-example of
people who guarded their time, and does not pretend to have solved it herself.

Godin's version of beginning is the sharpest formulation in the episode: "there's no such
thing as writer's block. Writer's block is real, but it does not exist." What it actually is,
he says, is fear of bad writing and of what the world will say about it — so the way through
is to do the bad writing, without shipping it. His model is Isaac Asimov, who typed for six
hours every morning whether or not it was any good, and looked through what was left at the
end of the shift; Godin's reading is that the subconscious works out that since it is going
to type anyway, it may as well type something good.

## A practice that produces pages

Seinfeld and Sanderson answer this one, and they answer it the same way: the output is a
by-product of a system, and the system is boring on purpose.

Seinfeld's unit is the **bounded session**. An open-ended writing session is "a ridiculous
torture to put on a human being's head" — his test case is hiring a trainer who says the
workout is open-ended, which nobody would accept. So: pick a duration, and the end of it is
the reward. "The alarm goes off, and you're done." When his daughter said she would write all
day, he told her nobody writes all day. He gives the same treatment to meditation — if
fifteen minutes doesn't work, do ten, the point being to find a length that will actually
happen. His other two lines are worth keeping: writing is "a game of tonnage", and you should
"learn to accept your mediocrity", because the people who are great are the ones who put in
the hours.

Sanderson's unit is **consistency across days**, with numbers attached. Nine writers in ten
do better writing regularly than in bursts; the one in ten is a binge writer who disappears
to a cabin for two months and doesn't write for the other ten, and he says he doesn't
understand that mode well. His own target is 2,000–2,500 new words a day, which he works out
to a theoretical 500,000 a year, minus the third of his time that goes to revision, so around
300,000 — and a *Stormlight Archive* book is eighteen months for that reason. His class asks
for 35,000 words over roughly a third of a year, i.e. a novel's length at that rate.

The mechanics he teaches are habit-stacking and environment: a specific place used for
little else, music reserved for writing, and attaching the session to a habit that already
exists — go to the gym, think about the writing there, come back and write for an hour. He
also gives exercises, but only as diagnosis: the person who keeps re-revising chapter three
instead of writing chapter four is told to draft longhand and type up yesterday's pages as
the way into today's, which physically separates the two activities.

Note that these two answers are the same shape at different scales — bound the session,
repeat the session — and that neither is about what to write.

## When the pages are bad

Lamott's claim about *Bird by Bird* is that it worked because it promised nothing: not that
finishing would get you published, and not that publication would heal anything, since
"all of that is an inside job." What she teaches is narrower than writing well. "I just teach
them to stop *not* writing" — keep your butt in the chair, write badly, and know that every
book you admire started as an unreadable draft. Her grandson, aged five, caught the scope
honestly: you said you'd teach them to write a book, but you only taught us how to write one
page.

The two images that carry the chapter are both about scale and both worth having. *Bird by
bird* is her father's instruction to her brother, who had a term paper on birds due Monday
and hadn't started on Saturday: read about chickadees, write a paragraph on chickadees, draw
a picture, then pelicans. E.L. Doctorow's is the one she calls the most profound advice she
can offer on any topic — writing is like driving at night with the headlights on, you can
only see a little way ahead, and you can make the whole journey that way.

On the critic itself she is more specific than the usual advice. Perfectionism is "the voice
of the oppressor", and *Bird by Bird*'s KFKD chapter is about a station that runs
twenty-four hours a day telling you how far short you are falling. Her husband Neal Allen's
practice, in *Shapes of Truth*, is not to remove the critic but to reassign it: thank it for
keeping you alive at six and seven by keeping you small and controllable, note that it is no
longer needed for that, then give it a new job — ethical consultant,
sent off to a library with a good chair, to be fetched when an ethical consultation is
actually wanted. Her honest caveat is that the left-hand speaker never turns off completely;
it turns down.

Karr's answer is about revision, and the number is the thing. She threw out roughly 1,200
pages of *Lit* — "finished pages too, that's not draft" — written over five or six years, on
a contract she was seven years late for. What she did about it was go to bed for two days,
watch television, and then call Don DeLillo to say she thought she was writing a bad book.
His reply was "Well, who doesn't?", and her conclusion from it is the useful part: every
writer she knows has written a bad book, so maybe this is a bad book, "but it's the book
that's standing in line to be written." She became willing to fail in order to say what
happened.

Her method underneath that is sensory rather than analytical. Her test on a sentence is a
list — can I make this less boring, more interesting, prettier, more colourful, more true —
and the way she gets material worth revising is to re-occupy a former self in a place:
the icy water from the under-dash air conditioner spilling onto her bare feet when her mother
turned right, the peaches from Arkansas, the vodka and orange juice. The exercise she teaches
from it is to pick a room you grew up in, get the smell first because smell is the oldest and
most emotional memory, then interrogate the body for what it can taste and touch, and finally
for what you wanted and what was in the way. The claim attached: as in trauma, the body
remembers — and it remembers pleasure and beauty as well.

Godin's contribution here is the one aimed at a specific person, since Ferriss is describing
his own hundreds of unpublished drafts. Two moves. The first is to challenge the premise:
*not good enough to publish — says who?* The real statement, he suggests, is that the writing
doesn't match the promise you'd like to make to the market you think you're in, which is a
different claim and not obviously one Ferriss is well placed to judge; hand 5,000 words to
someone whose actual skill is knowing when a thing is ready, and ask where it's thin and what
to cut. The second is a replacement for "just do it", which he reads as licence to be a hack:
**merely** do the work. The time spent narrating and catastrophising the work is not helping.
He is describing eight consecutive years of failing as an entrepreneur while dramatising the
problems, and the shift out of it as dropping the narrative rather than solving anything.

His last point is the one that generalises furthest. Twice he decided he had written the best
book he had in him and stopped. After *Permission Marketing* he sat in the dark for a year,
and what ended it was accidental: Gladwell, then unknown, sent him *The Tipping Point* for a
blurb, and reading it in one night he realised he had already been writing a book about how
ideas spread. He wrote it in twelve days, offered to throw it out if Gladwell objected, and
got a blessing and a foreword instead. What he took from it is that he could not write
*Permission Marketing* again — "that day was gone" — but he could write this one, and someone
would benefit.

After *Linchpin* he felt the same way again and did not get an accident. Six or nine months
later he decided the stopping had been selfish, which is where generosity comes in: not free
work, but spending emotional labour on somebody who could use a hand. Once there was a
specific person on the other end, "it wasn't about me anymore", and it got easier to merely
do the work.

## Where they disagree

Sanderson supplies the frame for reading the rest of the episode, which is a useful accident
of the edit. He opens his class by telling students they will have to learn when to ignore
him, because he is an authority standing at the front of a room and **survivorship bias means
he cannot actually tell which parts of what he did mattered**. He presents the material as a
toolbox that is deliberately self-contradictory.

His own example: Stephen King says don't outline and don't use a writing group; Orson Scott
Card says an outline is fundamentally vital. Using George R.R. Martin's terms, King is a
gardener — he plants two characters in conflict and finds out what happens, and says an
outline kills his motivation because the discovery has already been spent — and Card is an
architect, building the structure first and then polishing pieces of it. Sanderson's point is
that you cannot weight both equally and get anywhere; you can hybridise, but at some point
you pick one and test it on yourself.

The compilation contains at least three more contradictions of the same kind, none of them
flagged in the episode:

- **Bounded sessions vs. tonnage.** Seinfeld's hour with an alarm and a reward, against
  Asimov's six hours every morning regardless of quality. Both are systems; they are not the
  same prescription, and Godin's is much closer to Sanderson's daily quota than to Seinfeld's.
- **Consistency vs. the long collapse.** Sanderson's spreadsheet and daily word count against
  Karr taking five or six years and discarding 1,200 finished pages. Sanderson would call
  Karr the one-in-ten he doesn't understand; nothing in her account suggests a word count
  would have helped, and nothing in his suggests hers is a method that could be taught.
- **Finish what you start vs. throw it out.** Gilbert's rule is that abandoning is the
  failure mode. Karr's turning point was abandoning most of a book. The two are reconcilable —
  she stayed with the *project* and threw out the *pages* — but only if you are careful about
  what the unit is, and the episode doesn't do that work.

## What a compilation like this can't tell you

Worth recording, because the format flatters the material. Seven successful writers describing
the practice that preceded their success is a sample selected on the outcome; nobody in the
episode kept a daily word count for a decade and produced nothing, and those people exist.
Sanderson is the only one who says this out loud, and he says it about himself.

Two smaller things. The advice is about *practice*, almost never about the writing itself —
Karr's sentence test and her sensory exercise are the only craft content in the episode, and
Lamott is explicit that she teaches people to stop not-writing rather than to write well. And
the answers converge partly because the interviewer asked converging questions across the
original interviews; the four questions in the introduction are his, not theirs.

The common ground that survives all of that is fairly small, and it is the same three items in
every account: begin before you feel like it, bound the work so it can be repeated, and let
the first pass be bad. That third one is the one that connects to the rest of this notebook,
and it has its own page in [[separating-drafting-from-judging]].
