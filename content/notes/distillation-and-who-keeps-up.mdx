From the middle of [[rsi-roundtable-schulman-millidge-oneill|the Dwarkesh roundtable]]. It deserves its own
page because it is an answer to a question the notebook hasn't asked yet: with this much capital
behind a few labs, why isn't there one winner?

## The claim

John Schulman's answer: **distillation is the main force against centralisation.** "Anything that can be
learned through RL can be distilled very easily, because it's a small number of bits." If you can get
trajectories from a model that show a behaviour, you can copy it. Beren Millidge adds that continual
learning does not stop this, since a model that improves daily can be distilled daily.

The constraint is the **prompt distribution**. Distilling with supervised learning means prompting the
teacher with something, and it must be a wide distribution of realistic prompts. Schulman says that
even with full access and the chain of thought it is "non-trivial to distil all of the useful
capabilities".

## The router-data argument

Schulman says some Chinese companies are probably using proxy services that let users in China reach
blocked US frontier models, mostly for coding, and that those services collect and sell the data, which
gives "the perfect prompt distribution". He cites a news report for the existence of the proxy services,
not for the selling; the use for distillation is his inference ("probably").

Millidge's reply is that you can automate much of this: frontier pipelines start from seed prompts from
humans and real users and synthesise wide coverage from existing models, so humans need to supply fewer
bits as models improve. Dwarkesh doubts that: the valuable part is the whole multi-turn trace of a
user changing their mind. And it is, Millidge says, easier for the distiller than for the lab. "I want a
good politician" can be answered by asking a frontier model for a billion variations, where the first
lab had to find out what politicians do.

Frontier labs also buy data from the same data vendors, so a competitor can too.

## The puzzle that follows from it

Charlie O'Neill's version: Sonnet 5 and Opus 5 look "almost objectively worse" than GLM-5.3 and Kimi
K3, even though Anthropic had logit distillation from its larger internal model (he names Mythos)
and the environments to go with it. The first reply is the prompt distribution: the Chinese labs see
what users actually do. His inference: frontier labs may have little real advantage in RL environments
any more, and, in Dwarkesh's summary, **real-world deployment** matters more than the environment.

Schulman's explanation, which I find the more useful, is two axes of environment: **difficulty and
realism**. Hard puzzle-like tasks that are easy to verify are cheap to build ("the benchmaxxing
distribution"); realism (multi-turn work with a person and several objectives, rubrics, human
feedback) is what the lab has to push on to get good behaviour. Distilling naively matches the
teacher on the first and not the second. A student trained on a good realistic prompt
distribution can match the big model well; one trained only on easily verifiable tasks matches on
benchmarks and does worse on the broader distribution. He offers that as a possible explanation for the
smaller Anthropic models, while allowing that post-training is easy to get wrong in ways benchmarks
don't show, so a quirk may simply be a mistake. O'Neill's
version is that Opus 5 uses a lot of tokens because it behaves as if it is judging itself on
every step, without the "big model smell" to know when to stop.

## The cost: monoculture

Schulman: so many people distil from Claude that open-weight models "write the same way as Claude and
have the same tics", and RL has already cut output diversity. This seems to him "kind of concerning".
Millidge puts the blame on the data and the judge, not on RL or distillation as methods.

## How it fits

- It is the supply side of [[learning-from-deployment]]: deployment traces are valuable enough to
  be what people steal and sell, and the labs' own policy of not training on customers' data is
  what Millidge doubts for the labs and is certain does not hold for distillers.
- It explains why the same source, [[jev-system-one-models-diogo-almeida]], builds a company on
  synthetic data and refuses to train on traffic: the opposite bet.
- A router such as [[openrouter-seed-to-stripe|OpenRouter]] sits in the same position as the proxy
  services, with a stated policy of not seeing prompts unless an organisation opts in. That is a
  policy statement from one side of one interview, and nothing says the proxy services in the news
  report work the same way.
- It sharpens the question [[model-speciation-and-touching-the-weights]] raises: if copying a
  behaviour is that easy, speciation would be cheap to follow, and the harder thing is the prompt
  distribution that produces it.

## What is solid

The mechanism (RL results being few bits, copyable given prompts) is argued, and the guests mostly
agree. The router-data claim, the Sonnet/Opus versus GLM/Kimi comparison and the quality ranking are
opinions from three people who each have an interest in the open-model side, from a spoken
transcript. No numbers are given for any of it.
