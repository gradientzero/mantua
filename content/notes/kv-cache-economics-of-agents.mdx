Source: "(KV) Cache Rules Everything Around Me" by Diogo, on his Substack *Complete Skeptic*,
dated 2026-09-09 (`completeskeptic.com/p/kv-cache-rules-everything-around`), captured as a PDF
print of the page. Archived under `sources/2026-09/kv-cache-rules-everything-around-me/`. Most of
the post's charts are hidden under a subscribe overlay in the capture, so the figures below are the
ones stated in the text; the one chart that survives is the opening pie.

## The claim

An agent is a loop: read the context, emit a tool call, append the result, read it all again. With
a context of N tokens and T tool calls you process about N × T input tokens to produce a small
number of output tokens, and since N grows every turn, **cost grows quadratically with session
length**. Most of those input tokens are cache reads, charged at a discount but charged every
time.

His numbers, from a small simulator replaying Nvidia's Open-SWE-Traces at Fable 5 prices:

- **Output tokens are 9–18% of the bill.** The opening chart puts it at cache reads 67%, output
  roughly 18%, fresh input 12%, cache-write premium 3% — against a "what devs think" pie of
  output 75%, input 19%.
- **Hit rate is a large lever.** The whole dataset costs $1.07M at a perfect hit rate and $1.97M
  at Claude Code's reported 89% — 84% more. Going from 95% to 98% saves about 17%.
- Martin Alderson's independent model of a 100-turn session from 60k tokens of context lands in
  the same place: cache reads about 76% of an Opus 5 bill.

## Why the provider barely pays for it

The argument has three steps, and it is the part worth keeping even if the prices move:

1. **Prefill costs compute.** Reading the prompt is one parallel forward pass, cost proportional
   to tokens. The KV cache *is* the saved result of that pass; a cache read is input that needs no
   compute.
2. **Decode costs memory bandwidth.** Every generated token attends over the whole KV cache, so
   it reads the context out of HBM once per output token. That is why output is priced several
   times above input.
3. **A tool call is just a pause.** The cache stays resident while the tool runs. Nothing is
   recomputed; the bytes only have to be kept.

So, his claim: the real cost of reading the cache is already paid inside the output-token price,
and the separate charge for cache reads is mostly margin. He points at two provider behaviours as
corroboration — cache reads do not count against input rate limits on the Claude API, and OpenAI
ran automatic prefix caching for over a year without a write premium, adding the 1.25× only with
GPT-5.6.

This lines up with [[inference-engineering-baseten|the Baseten interview]], seen from the other
side of the counter: Kiely's first step for any long request is cache-aware routing, because
prefill is the expensive part and a hit skips it. The provider organises its fleet around the
cache; the customer's bill is organised around charging for it.

## What follows from it

**Routing a step to a cheaper model usually costs more.** A KV cache is not shared between models,
so the moment a second model touches the context it pays to prefill all of it. His worked example —
a 100k-token Opus 5 session, one step of 1k output and 3k tool result, done by Opus or handed to
Sonnet and back — makes the "easy" step almost 3× more expensive, done by the weaker model. A
per-request router pays that on every switch, and worst on the longest contexts.

**Subagents mostly don't pay off either — if they need the parent's context.** Handing the full
context to a second model means writing it into a second cache. The exception, in his footnote, is
the useful one: a subagent with a tiny brief ("search the codebase for X, report back in 200
tokens") never inherits the parent's context, and the 50k tokens of grep output it reads never
enter the parent's. That makes N smaller for every later turn, which is the only thing in this
account that bends the quadratic.

**The subscription "subsidy" may not be one.** SemiAnalysis found the $200/month plans can yield
$8,000 (Anthropic) to $14,000 (OpenAI) a month of API-equivalent tokens — a 40–70× subsidy at list
price. If 90%+ of a coding agent's tokens are cache reads and those are nearly free to serve, the
list price is the wrong denominator.

**Self-hosting makes more sense for agents than for chat.** Running open weights yourself is the
only way to keep the cheapness of the cache instead of paying list price for it. He hedges this
himself: Fable 5.1 cut cache reads from $1.00 to $0.25 per million, so the gap may keep closing.

**What to measure**: cache hit rate and the number of tool calls. On his account the tool-call
count matters far more than the tokens per call.

## How it lands against what is here

**It sharpens the routing question on [[benchmarking-your-own-agent-spend]].** DoorDash's stated
plan is to route cheap tasks to open-weight models and pay frontier prices only where they buy
something. That survives this argument only if the routing is *per task* — a fresh session with its
own context — and not per step inside a session. Routing per step is exactly the case he shows
losing. The same distinction applies to [[serving-slms-on-a-desktop-gpu|the desktop under the
desk]]: sending whole jobs to the local model is fine; switching models mid-conversation is the
expensive move. And the self-hosting argument is a second reason for that box to exist, separate
from privacy: an agent loop run locally keeps the cache discount instead of paying for it.

**It puts a price on the parallelism decision in [[command-over-tokens]].** Many short runs in
flight, or a fleet of 20–50 subagents as in [[gpt-6-astra-automated-ai-engineer|the GPT-6 Astra
writeup]], is cheap or expensive depending on one thing the usual discussion skips: how much context
each worker inherits. Narrow briefs and short reports are the cheap version, and also the better
harness, since they keep the parent's context small. Forking a full context twenty times is paying
for twenty caches.

**It gives the long-running-agent material a cost curve.** The pages on
[[keeping-an-agent-running|keeping an agent running]] and
[[harness-design-for-long-running-coding-agents|Anthropic's long-running harness]] weigh session
length against the context window and the model's behaviour near its limit. Quadratic cost is a
third reason, independent of both, to prefer a context reset from a written handoff over one
ever-growing session — the harness writeup arrives at resets for behavioural reasons, and this is
the billing reason for the same move.

## Where it thins out

**"Keeping bytes around costs nothing" is the weak step.** Resident KV cache does not use compute
or bandwidth while it sits there, but it uses HBM *capacity*, and capacity is what caps batch size
— which is what decode throughput, and so the provider's economics, actually depend on. A long
context parked during a slow tool call is memory that is not serving anyone else. The provider can
offload it to CPU memory or disk, and the Baseten interview says that offloading is where the
industry is heading, but the move costs something. One commenter on the post makes the market
version of the objection: if cache reads really cost nothing, some lab would price them at zero and
take share. None has.

**The headline percentages depend on the price sheet.** With output priced 5× input and cache reads
at a tenth of input, the split follows from the trace shape; a different ratio moves it. The
direction is robust — long agent sessions are input-dominated — and the exact 67% is not.

**"Mostly margin" is an inference, not a disclosure.** The rate-limit and pricing-history points
are consistent with it, and neither is a cost figure. The same goes for the step from "cache reads
are cheap" to "this is why inference margins are rising"; it is plausible and it is not shown.

**Hit rates are reported, not measured here.** He quotes 89% and 95% for Claude Code from different
people and says to take both with salt.

**He has a stake.** The post ends by hinting at "a new kind of foundation model" that escapes the
KV cache and invites people to get in touch. That does not change the arithmetic, which anyone can
redo, but it is the reason the conclusion is framed as a tyranny to escape.

## For this notebook

The practical takeaway is small and checkable: this repo's own sessions have a hit rate and a
