Two days before Andy Fang described DashBench on NoPriors as a benchmark that scores models
and harnesses together, the team that built it published how. It is the most detailed public
account in this notebook of *measuring* an agent rather than building one, and the part worth
keeping is the methodology, not the leaderboard.

One correction to fold in first: the interview left the impression of a benchmark over
coding tasks generally. It isn't. DashBench measures a **code reviewer**, on replayed
historical pull requests. Benchmarking coding *agents* on real tasks is named at the end of
the post as the next thing, not something they have.

## Why acceptance rate lies

The argument that makes the post worth reading, and it generalises past code review.

The obvious metric — did the author accept the comment, did they act on it — is the main
metric most review tools use. Put it in a confusion matrix and it fills exactly two cells.
Accepted books a true positive; rejected books a false positive. **False negatives and true
negatives are structurally unobservable**: acceptance telemetry cannot record the bug the
reviewer never mentioned, and it cannot record the clean file where silence was the correct
answer. So the metric tells you something happened. It cannot tell you whether the review
was right, or what it missed.

And the two cells it does fill are booked against a label that assumes the author is ground
truth. They are not. Authors accept and reject for product and workflow reasons — timing,
urgency, ownership, how invasive the fix is, whether the issue was already handled another
way — and in DoorDash's own disagreement audits both the humans and the agentic verifier
were caught making mistakes. Their formulation: acceptance is valuable **product telemetry
and a weak benchmark label**, and the job is to separate *was accepted* from *was real*.

That is the same failure this notebook has already recorded one level up. "Yeah, it works
okay" from the team using the tool is [[benchmarking-your-own-agent-spend|not a measurement]];
an agent asked to grade its own output [[generator-evaluator-loops|praises it]]. Here the
generator's seat is occupied by the engineer whose PR is being reviewed, and the result is
the same shape: the most available signal is the one with a stake in the answer.

## Labels that don't rest on one source

The fix is not a better single metric. It is triangulation across several flawed ones, with
none promoted to ground truth:

- the engineers who wrote the PRs annotate candidate findings by hand;
- the original candidate findings from the review run;
- an LLM judge.

Where the three disagree, a human re-reads the evidence and resolves it, and **the resolved
disagreements become calibration data for the judge**. The judge stays in the loop as a
calibrated signal rather than an oracle. Missed real clusters are booked as false negatives
after adjudication — which is how the two cells acceptance can't reach get filled at all.

The case selection is designed against specific failure modes rather than for coverage:
historical PRs with real findings and enough context to replay faithfully; **benign PRs with
close to zero real findings**, because a reviewer that is loud on clean code is its own
failure mode and nothing else in the pipeline would catch it; and PRs that were later
reverted or hotfixed, to test whether genuine regressions get caught before merge. Roughly
1,000 raw candidates narrowed to a 105-case valid report.

This is the most direct answer anyone in this notebook has given to *what checks the check?*
— the question [[generator-evaluator-loops]] raises three separate times and leaves open. The
answer is not a better judge. It is refusing to have one, plus a standing budget of human
adjudication to keep the judge honest. Expensive, and the post doesn't say how expensive.

## The staged architecture, and why it's a clean eval surface

The production reviewer separates *noticing* from *verifying*: a lead scout reads the change
and flags suspicious areas, deep reviewers investigate the strongest leads and drop the
claims that don't survive. That is the generator–evaluator split applied to review itself,
with the twist that the second stage exists to **kill** findings rather than to grade an
artifact — precision-over-recall by construction.

The reason it matters for measurement is incidental and useful: because the workflow is
model-agnostic, every component is a variable that can be held fixed or moved on its own —
scout model, reviewer model, context policy, tool policy, runtime budget. So the question
stops being "which model is best" and becomes: at this architecture, context policy, tool
policy and budget, what tradeoff between coverage, precision, cost and latency do we get,
and where does it fail. Their line: best is meaningless until you say best *at what*, on
*which* cases, at *what* cost.

Note what they refuse to hold constant. The harness is part of what is being measured, so
profiles differ in runner kind, tool surface, context mechanics and provider limits, and
cost, latency, timeouts and retries are **recorded as results rather than controlled away**.
The unit is the model *and* its scaffolding — the unit [[agentic-engineering|this cluster]]
argues for, here with the tables to go with it.

## The numbers

Staging buys coverage and the price is visible. On the 105-case report:

| System | Real findings | Weighted precision | Weighted recall | Cost / PR | Latency / PR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Production (Sonnet 4.6 high scout + Opus 4.8 high reviewer) | 504 | 87.0% | 53.6% | $3.91 | 725s |
| No scout + GPT 5.5 high | 164 | 84.1% | 30.7% | $0.75 | 170s |
| No scout + Opus 4.8 high | 115 | 89.8% | 20.2% | $0.65 | 113s |

Weighting is by severity — critical 4, high 2, medium 1, low 0.5 — which is the decision that
makes the headline number mean anything, and it is the same move as
[[self-improving-agents-from-production-feedback|the tax agents' threshold buckets]]: score
the thing the user actually cares about, not the mean.

The model-mix table is the more interesting artifact, because nothing dominates. Best
weighted recall and F1: Kimi K2.6 scout + Fable 5 reviewer, 65.2% / 75.3% at $3.81. Best
weighted precision: Composer 2.5 scout + GPT 5.5 medium, 92.2% — at 18.0% recall. Cheapest:
no-scout Opus 4.8 at $0.65, giving up most of the coverage. Their own production
configuration comes **second** on recall and F1, which is a small mark of credibility: they
published a table their own system doesn't top.

Severity splits it again. Kimi + Fable leads critical (80.0% vs 62.5%), high and medium;
the production pair covers more of the low tail. The cheap single-pass GPT 5.5 row is weak
overall — 4.2% recall on low — and still respectable on high/critical, which is a genuinely
useful thing to know if you are routing by cost. Configurations that look close on one
number miss entirely different kinds of issue.

## Four lessons, and the one I'd keep

Their own summary, compressed:

**Trust must be earned** — no single signal is ground truth, and each of the three was wrong
often enough that leaning on any one would have quietly poisoned the scores.

**Humans don't scale** — labeller attention degrades fast on complex tasks, even when the
labeller wrote the PR. AI judging is what makes the scale tractable at all.

**Variance is a feature.** The one I'd keep. LLMs are non-deterministic, so repeated runs of
the same agent surface *additional valid findings* — which means **a single run understates
an agent's coverage**, and honest scoring requires running repeatedly and aggregating. Same
for the judge: deterministic matching is stable and misses semantic equivalence; an LLM judge
reasons better and needs calibration, audit sets and stable rubrics. The whole measurement
stack is stochastic and the work is designing for that rather than pretending it away. This
is a concrete, cheap correction to anything I build — a rubric run once is a lower bound, and
I would not have known to say so.

**When one metric can't work, look to many.** Weighted precision, recall, F1, not-real
findings, high/critical recall, latency and cost all move independently.

## Where it thins out

**Recall is measured against what the pool found.** The severity denominator is the *union
of adjudicated real clusters* across configurations — 40 critical, 136 high, 271 medium, 385
low. A bug that no configuration in the pool surfaced, and that no annotator noticed, is not
a false negative for anybody; it is invisible. So these recall figures are recall against
collective discovery, not against the true defect population. The post is clear about the
construction and does not draw attention to what it implies.

**The judge grades the judge's students.** Calibrated on resolved disagreements from the
same case set, then used to score systems on it. That is the honest version of the loop, but
it is still a loop, and the audit budget is the only thing outside it. Their answer for the
bias half — moving from a single judge to an **agentic jury** — is listed under what's next,
which is also the cross-model evaluator experiment that
[[building-a-generator-evaluator-harness-plan|TandemKit asserts and nobody has measured]].

**No spread, no repetition count.** Given that "variance is a feature" is one of their own
four lessons, the tables carry no error bars and no statement of how many runs each
configuration got. A 105-case cut with severity tiers down to 40 critical clusters is small
enough that the gap between two adjacent rows may be noise.

**Nothing about what it cost to build.** Roughly 1,000 candidate PRs curated, engineers
annotating their own PRs by hand, a standing disagreement-adjudication pass. That is the
part a one-person setup has to price and the post does not price it.

## What transfers

Not the benchmark. The sequence: the reviewer had to exist and be used before there was
anything to measure, and the measurement layer was built *after* the product metric turned
out to point at the wrong target. Three things scale down to a notebook or a single
repository —

- **Name the cells you cannot observe.** Before trusting any metric, draw the confusion
  matrix and ask which quadrants it can populate. `npm run build` here fills exactly one:
  it catches schema violations, and it is silent about every note that is wrong in a way the
  schema permits.
- **Keep benign cases in the set.** Restraint is measurable only if some cases are clean.
  The equivalent for [[linting-as-agent-guardrail|/lint here]] would be pages known to be
  fine, to catch a lint pass that manufactures findings to look useful.
- **Run it more than once.** Aggregate across runs before believing a coverage number.
